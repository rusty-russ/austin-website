About

Austin is a postdoctoral research associate in the Gross Laboratory at the University of Cincinnati, where he studies how sensory systems evolve — presently through taste-receptor diversification in Astyanax mexicanus, the Mexican cavefish, whose surface- and cave-dwelling morphotypes make it a natural experiment in adaptation to permanent darkness.

His path here has run through salamander plots in Ohio's second-growth forests, shorebird nests on the Massachusetts coast, hellbender streams in Appalachia, and five field seasons banding chickadees along a Pennsylvania hybrid zone — a route that keeps circling back to the same question: how do microbes, genes, and environment interact to shape which organisms an ecosystem keeps, and how they sense the world around them?

He completed his Ph.D. in Biology at Lehigh University under Dr. Amber M. Rice, where his dissertation examined gut microbiome diversity in hybridizing Black-capped and Carolina Chickadees, and he currently also teaches Animal Behavior as an adjunct instructor at Thomas More University. Outside the lab, he coaches high school soccer — having captained his own teams through high school and college — and works to bring hands-on science to classrooms and outreach events around greater Cincinnati.

Current work

Gross Lab · University of Cincinnati

Taste receptor evolution in Astyanax mexicanus

How does a single species, having colonized total darkness on multiple independent occasions, rewrite its own sense of taste?

Astyanax mexicanus exists as both a river-dwelling surface fish and, independently across at least thirty cave populations in Mexico, a blind, depigmented cavefish — making it one of the best natural systems for studying repeated, rapid adaptation. Austin is mapping how T1R and T2R taste receptor genes diverge in expression between these morphotypes, using RNA-seq (Salmon quantification, DESeq2 differential expression) alongside in situ hybridization, hybridization chain reaction (HCR), and multi-antibody immunofluorescence panels paired with whole-mount DEEP-Clear tissue clearing to visualize receptor expression across gill, craniofacial, and extraoral tissue.

The goal is to connect gene expression differences to the anatomical and ecological shifts that define cave adaptation — linking genotype, sensory anatomy, and the selective pressures of a lightless environment.

Pollution & microbiome, Ohio River corridor

A developing research line examining how pollution gradients along the Ohio River affect fitness and gut microbiome composition in avian and invertebrate host communities — combining long-term survival monitoring, heavy-metal analysis from feather collections, and microbiome characterization to link contaminant exposure to physiological outcomes.

Salamander skin microbiomes

A return to undergraduate roots: citizen-science-integrated, long-term monitoring of regional salamander populations — spotted, eastern tiger, and red-backed — pairing population genetics with skin microbiome profiling to understand how microbial symbionts shape amphibian resilience to environmental change.
